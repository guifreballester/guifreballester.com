## TL;DR

In Twilio's Video business unit, our largest accounts — the ones driving most of the revenue — were on the verge of churning. I worked directly with them to find what was actually blocking them, shipped a custom capability that solved it, and kept them on the platform. Then I generalized that same capability into the core product so every customer benefited. Separately, I spotted the post-COVID streaming gap and helped take Twilio Live 0→1.

## Context

Twilio Video gave developers real-time video as an API, and a handful of enterprise accounts represented an outsized share of the unit's revenue. Post-COVID demand was high, but those top accounts had outgrown what the standard product did — and losing any one of them would have hit the business unit hard.

## Problem

- A few large accounts drove most of the revenue, and they were at risk of leaving.
- They needed a capability the standard product didn't offer.
- Building one-off features for whales doesn't scale — and fragments the roadmap.
- The unit had to retain the revenue without turning into a custom-dev shop.

## What I Did

1. **Got close to the at-risk accounts**: I worked directly with the largest customers to find the real blocker behind the churn risk — the underlying need, not the symptom.
2. **Shipped a custom capability**: I scoped and delivered what those accounts needed to stay, prioritized against the revenue at risk.
3. **Generalized it into the core product**: instead of leaving it as bespoke work, I productized the capability so it shipped to the whole customer base — turning a retention save into a feature every customer could adopt.
4. **Spotted the streaming gap (Twilio Live)**: separately, I saw competitors were all focused on meetings (many-to-many) while no one served streaming at scale (one-to-many), and helped bring Twilio Live 0→1 — it reached ~$200K MRR for Twilio.

## Key Decisions

### Solve for the Whale, Then Productize
The instinct under churn pressure is a one-off fix. I built for the at-risk accounts first to stop the bleeding, but designed the capability so it could be generalized — so the work paid off across the entire base, not just the accounts that demanded it.

### Retention Is a Product Problem
These accounts weren't leaving over price or support — they were leaving over a missing capability. I treated retention as a roadmap input, not a CS escalation.

### Find Gaps, Not Competition (Twilio Live)
Rather than compete head-to-head on meetings, I went after the underserved streaming-at-scale market.

## Lessons Learned

1. **Retention is a roadmap signal.** When your biggest accounts threaten to churn, what they're missing is often the most valuable thing you can build next.
2. **Build for the whale, ship for everyone.** Custom work for a top account is only worth it if you design it to generalize — then one save becomes a platform feature.
3. **Look for gaps, not competition.** The biggest 0→1 openings are the segments everyone else skipped.
